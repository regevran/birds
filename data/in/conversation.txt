I think this makes the project substantially better. You're designing not just a checklist, but a **printed learning aid that can be read from a distance and gradually used to learn bird taxonomy**. That changes how I'd approach the layout.

Here is what I'd recommend.

## 1. One family per page

Agreed. A family should never be split across pages, even if it contains only one species. This gives each page a biological meaning rather than an arbitrary page-length limit.

It also makes the pages modular: you can print them again, rearrange them, or put related families together on a wall.

## 2. Optimize for readability, not page count

I'd make the Latin and Hebrew names fit on a single line, with no wrapping or truncation.

For an A4 page, I'd start with these design targets:

- **One column:** Latin name on the left, Hebrew name right-aligned on the right.
- **Font size:** approximately 16–18 pt for the names, then adjust based on the longest names.
- **Spacing:** generous vertical spacing between entries, especially if the pages will be displayed on a wall.
- **Two columns:** only when a family has enough species that two columns remain comfortably readable. We shouldn't force two columns just to save paper.

A single-column page with 20 species, for instance, may be more useful than a two-column page with 40 species if your primary goal is reading from a distance.

## 3. Make the taxonomy visible

I'd structure each page like this:

The family name should be prominent, with the Hebrew family name and perhaps a short English gloss beneath it. The genus would remain part of every full Latin species name. I would avoid repeating a separate genus label beside every bird, since that would add clutter.

## 4. A taxonomic detail worth getting right

We should settle on one authoritative classification before generating the pages. Families and their placement can differ between checklist versions, and scientific names occasionally change.

I'd use a recognized modern world-bird taxonomy consistently, cross-check it against the Israeli species list, and record the taxonomy version used. That way, the family pages won't mix incompatible classifications.

We should also distinguish between:

- **Species recorded in Israel** — including rare vagrants.
- **Regular visitors and residents** — birds you have a reasonable chance of encountering.
- **Taxonomic completeness** — including every species in each selected family that occurs in Israel, not just familiar examples.

These distinctions matter because a family page with one Israeli species should contain one entry, not every species in that family worldwide.

**My overall recommendation:** prioritize one family per page, legibility at a distance, and taxonomic accuracy. Let the total number of pages emerge from those requirements rather than imposing a page limit. I won't prepare the document yet.

## 2. The genus should be the primary learning unit

This is the more important insight.

For bird ringing, you're preparing yourself to identify and communicate a bird's taxonomic identity in the field. A genus-oriented layout helps you build that mental vocabulary.

For example, you would learn to associate *Streptopelia* with its Hebrew name and then learn the species within that genus. The genus becomes a recognizable unit rather than just the first word of a Latin name.

I'd therefore use this hierarchy:

- **Page:** one genus.
- **Page heading:** the scientific genus name, prominently displayed.
- **Entries:** all species of that genus recorded in Israel, with Latin and Hebrew names side by side.
- **Family:** a secondary classification, included as a small heading or reference to help you understand the biological relationships.

This also means a genus with one recorded species gets its own page, just as you proposed for families earlier.

## 3. One taxonomic distinction to keep in mind

The genus is the right focus for your stated goal, but a ringing identification may require more than a genus. Depending on the species and the ringing scheme, the recorded identification can be at species level, or sometimes only at a higher taxonomic level when the bird cannot be identified more precisely.

So I'd build the learning material to teach **genus first, species second**, without suggesting that genus alone is always the final identification.

Overall, I think this is a better design than the original family-based list. It aligns the physical organization of the pages with the terminology you want to recall during ringing, while retaining enough taxonomy to understand how the genera fit together.
